# Before anyone hops in the trench: what two October OSHA cases teach about planning

*Suggested URL: /blog/trench-safety-job-hazard-analysis · about 850 words*

**Meta description:** Two OSHA trench cases from October 2026 show what happens when nobody plans for a cave-in. Here are OSHA's trench rules in plain English and how a 5-minute JHA covers them.

---

Trenches look harmless. It's just a ditch, right? You've dug a hundred of them.

But a trench wall can let go with no warning, and when it does, there's no time to climb out. This October, OSHA put out two news releases that show exactly how that plays out, and both came down to the same thing: nobody planned for the dirt.

## Case 1: a fatal cave-in in Pennsylvania

On October 7, 2026, OSHA announced citations against J.D. Wagoner Excavating, an excavation contractor in Chambersburg, Pennsylvania. According to [OSHA's release](https://www.osha.gov/news/newsreleases/philadelphia/20261007), workers were installing a storm sewer in a trench about 12 feet deep that wasn't properly protected. Part of the trench wall failed, and a young worker was buried and killed.

OSHA cited the company for:

- **Willful:** not protecting workers from cave-ins.
- **Repeat:** not having a competent person inspect the trench daily, and not removing workers from hazardous conditions.
- **Serious:** no hazard prevention training, no safe way out of the trench, materials closer than two feet to the edge, and loose side materials that could roll in.

Proposed penalties: **$254,884.** (The company can contest the citations, and OSHA notes that case details can change.)

## Case 2: repeat trench violations in Illinois

Two weeks later, [OSHA cited Always Underground LLC](https://www.osha.gov/news/newsreleases/chicago/20261022), a utility construction company in Frankfort, Illinois. Under OSHA's trenching emphasis program, inspectors saw employees working in a trench deeper than eight feet with no protective system.

The company got three **repeat** violations (no safe exit, no cave-in protection, and dirt and equipment closer than two feet to the edge) and one **serious** violation for missing hard hats. Proposed penalties: **$380,664.**

Nobody was reported hurt in that one. That's the good news. The not-so-good news is that "repeat" means OSHA had cited the same problems before.

## OSHA's trench rules in plain English

Here's what [OSHA's trenching fact sheet](https://obis.osha.gov/Publications/trench_excavation_fs.html) says, without the legalese:

1. **5 feet or deeper?** You need a protective system: sloping the walls, shoring, or a trench box (shield).
2. **20 feet or deeper?** That protective system has to be designed by a registered professional engineer.
3. **4 feet or deeper?** Workers need a safe way out, like a ladder or ramp, **within 25 feet** of every worker.
4. **Keep loads back.** Dirt piles (spoils), tools and equipment stay **at least 2 feet** from the edge.
5. **Inspect daily.** A competent person checks the trench every day and whenever conditions change, before anyone goes in.

Notice something? Almost every citation in both cases lines up with one of those five rules.

## Where a JHA fits

A [Job Hazard Analysis](https://obis.osha.gov/Publications/osha3071.html) is just a plan for the dangerous parts of the job, written down before work starts. For a trench, it takes about five minutes at the tailgate:

| Job step | Hazard | Risk | Controls |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dig trench to 6 ft | Cave-in | Critical | Trench box or sloping; nobody in until it's in place |
| Enter and exit trench | Trapped with no way out | High | Ladder within 25 ft of every worker |
| Stockpile spoils | Dirt or gear rolling in | High | Keep everything 2 ft back from the edge |
| Daily start | Walls changed overnight (rain, vibration) | High | Competent person inspects before entry |
| Work near edge | Falling objects | Medium | Hard hats on |

That's it. Five rows. And each row answers a question that, in October's cases, nobody answered in time.

The JHA also does something a toolbox talk can't: it leaves a signed, dated record that the crew talked about the cave-in risk and agreed on the controls. If conditions change, you update the plan, not just your memory.
